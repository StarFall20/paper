### B6 Agreement at several diagnostic points

Fix the 32 labelled choices and the predictor in article Table 1 and set the correction weight to one. Take any finite list of coefficient vectors and any finite derivative order at each vector. There are three equal-weight auxiliary samples of the same size for which the corrected objective values, requested derivatives and empirical score covariances agree at every listed vector. Their corrected targets are interior, on the boundary and exterior, respectively. The labelled logit remains finite.

For each auxiliary sample, put half the rows at $x=0$ with equal counts at $z=-1$ and $z=1$. Give the remaining rows symmetric contrasts $x=±u$ with $z=0$, where $0<u<a$ and $a=3/4$. For each requested diagnostic, average its contribution over the two signs of $u$. The resulting functions are even and real analytic in $u$ on an interval containing $[-a,a]$. This applies to the loss, its derivatives and the uncentred products of the two score coordinates. Include both mean score coordinates to obtain the empirical covariance. The labelled terms are fixed. Constant diagnostic functions agree automatically, and it suffices to retain functions $f_1,…,f_r$ that form a basis modulo constants.

The functions $f_1',…,f_r',1$ are linearly independent on $(0,a)$. A linear relation extends to zero, where every $f_j'$ vanishes. Its coefficient on the constant must be zero. Integrating the remaining relation would give a constant linear combination of the basis functions. Choose $r+1$ points $u_i$ in $(0,a)$ where the columns of their evaluations form a nonsingular matrix. Such points exist because the evaluation vectors span the full space by independence. Use the magnitudes $u_i$ and $a-u_i$, with both signs. Their mean magnitude is $a/2=3/8$. The complete sample has $8(r+1)$ rows, including the fixed profiles.

Hold the magnitudes $a-u_i$ fixed. As the $r+1$ other magnitudes vary, the map from them to the sums of the $f_j$ and the sum of the magnitudes has the nonsingular matrix above as its derivative. The inverse function theorem gives two perturbations that hold all diagnostic sums equal and move the mean magnitude to opposite sides of $3/8$. Sufficiently small perturbations keep every node in $(0,a)$ and every row at its original weight.

For all three samples, the corrected moment is $(1/2,3/32)^T$. By symmetry the optimal intercept at each slope is zero. If $m_u$ is the mean magnitude of the nonzero pairs, the slope score increases strictly from $-3/32$ at zero to $m_u/4-3/32$. The opposite direction has recession $m_u/4+3/32$. Thus the two perturbations and their common baseline give the three existence outcomes. The equal-weight samples can all occur with positive probability under one symmetric population whose conditional choice probability is $g(x,z)$. The fitted human coefficient is zero in this population.

A 48-row auxiliary example matches values, gradients, Hessians and covariances at zero and at the common first Newton point, whose slope is 3.776118. It also matches third derivatives at zero. Its baseline uses $u_i$ equal to 0.04, 0.12, 0.23, 0.37, 0.55 and 0.72 and their six complements. For the other two designs the sum of the six free magnitudes changes by $10^{-6}$ in each direction. The positive-slope recession rates are approximately $2.0833\times10^{-8}$, zero and $-2.0833\times10^{-8}$. The finite design has slope approximately 367.840.
