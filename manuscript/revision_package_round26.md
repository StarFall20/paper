## Recommended title

**Testing Utility-Basis Sufficiency in Discrete Choice Experiments: A Support-Complete Fibre Design**

The title states the estimand and the design object. Nuisance balancing remains
a control condition in the methods; it does not compete with the main claim in
the title.

## Recommended abstract

Discrete-choice models are often evaluated on the support generated by a
standard efficient design. That support can estimate a candidate utility basis
precisely while leaving its discarded raw coordinates untestable. We formulate
utility-basis sufficiency as the conditional relation
\(Y\perp X\mid\phi(X)\), where \(\phi\) is the complete candidate menu
prediction, and construct feasible discrete fibres on which \(\phi\) is fixed.
For a finite choice-experiment support, a support-complete contrast space spans
every non-constant response pattern inside each non-singleton fibre. Its rank
and smallest eigenvalue provide a pre-outcome certificate of structural
visibility; statistical power is calculated separately from the response
metric and effect size. Nuisance-matched reflected tasks hold comparison load
and display changes fixed, and a respondent-level assignment supplies the
randomization reference. On a (3\times3\) counterexample, a candidate-
precision endpoint design has residual rank zero, whereas a support-complete
design has rank four and minimum exposure eigenvalue 0.1222. In a 100-
replication benchmark with 400 respondents, a departure constructed to be
orthogonal to a two-feature residual dictionary is detected in .89 of
support-complete runs and .08 of dictionary runs at the same respondent
budget; null rejection is .01 and .03. The smart-device grid supplies a
feasible application design, while a public Swissmetro run verifies the
implementation as an observational external check. The contribution is a
finite-support, design-based audit of representation sufficiency, with clear
stop rules for singleton fibres, missing assignment, and process imbalance.

## Main contribution paragraph

The paper asks a design question that ordinary fit comparisons leave open:
does the candidate summary retain all choice-relevant information on the
support that the experiment actually creates? The answer is obtained by
holding the complete candidate menu prediction fixed and allocating support
to every non-constant profile contrast within each feasible fibre. A zero rank
is a structural blind spot; a positive rank with a small eigenvalue is a
power problem; a rejection is a relation violation on the declared support.
This separates representation refutability from parameter precision and from
the choice of a particular rival utility term.

## Evidence hierarchy

1. The finite theorem and (3\times3) rank counterexample establish the
   structural distinction.
2. The hidden-direction benchmark demonstrates the gain over a hand-picked
   dictionary under a fixed respondent budget.
3. The smart-device enumeration establishes that the required fibres can be
   constructed in the substantive attribute space.
4. The Swissmetro analysis verifies the code on a real multinomial DCE but is
   labelled observational because the public file lacks a candidate-preserving
   assignment variable.
5. A randomized paired DCE remains the confirmation required for a human-data
   claim. Until then, the paper is a method-and-design contribution with an
   explicit external implementation check.

## Claims to remove

- “first” claims about invariance or conditional randomization;
- claims that a rejection identifies a unique omitted attribute or mechanism;
- claims of nonparametric sufficiency on a continuous attribute domain;
- claims that public Swissmetro validates the randomized fibre estimand;
- separate headline labels for BMST, UFIT, FQC, parity, or policy weighting.

The paper should use one framework name and present the parity and nuisance
controls as design constructions inside it.
