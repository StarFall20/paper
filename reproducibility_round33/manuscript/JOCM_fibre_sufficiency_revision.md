# Testing Utility Basis Sufficiency in Discrete Choice Experiments

A support complete fibre design and finite support audit

Working manuscript draft

Unspecified authors

Abstract. Choice-model diagnostics often evaluate a fitted utility specification on the support generated by an efficient design. A design can estimate a reduced utility basis precisely while leaving the raw coordinates discarded by that basis untestable. This paper formulates utility-basis sufficiency as the finite-support relation Y ⟂ X | φ(X), where φ(X) is the complete candidate menu summary and the response function given that summary remains unrestricted. We construct feasible non-singleton fibres that hold φ fixed, then span every non-constant response contrast inside each declared finite fibre. The resulting support-complete exposure matrix gives a rank and eigenvalue certificate before outcomes are collected. A 3 × 3 counterexample has candidate-summary variance 4.00 and exposure rank 0 under an endpoint precision design, while a support-complete allocation has rank 4 and minimum exposure eigenvalue 0.1222. In a 500-replication comparator benchmark, null rejection is 0.046 for the support-complete score, 0.046 for generalized CMH, 0.046 for the complete-fibre LR, and 0.042 for the exploratory held-out ML score; the hidden direction is rejected in 1.000, 1.000, 1.000, and 0.998 of runs, respectively. The generalized CMH score, complete-fibre likelihood ratio, and held-out ML residual score are reported as inferential comparators. The contribution is a pre-outcome support audit and repair rule, positioned explicitly against lack-of-fit and model-discrimination design criteria. A public Swissmetro run remains an implementation check; public observational data do not identify the candidate-preserving causal relation.

Keywords: discrete choice experiments; utility basis; conditional sufficiency; experimental design; model criticism; support coverage; multinomial choice

## 1 Introduction

Discrete choice experiments estimate preference models from a finite set of menus. Researchers then use those models to compare products, evaluate attribute changes, and simulate policies. The design that is efficient for estimating a reduced utility index need not be informative about whether raw attributes discarded by that index still affect choices.

This paper studies that design problem directly. Let X be the complete raw menu and let φ(X) be a prespecified candidate summary. The target null is Y ⟂ X | φ(X). It allows any choice-probability surface indexed by φ. A failure means that the coarsening map has discarded information on the tested support; functional-form questions inside φ remain separate.

The construction uses fibres: sets of feasible menus with identical φ. Randomly assigning raw profiles within a fibre creates a comparison for which every candidate coefficient vector gives the same candidate menu prediction. The support-complete certificate then asks whether the design spans the full non-constant response space within each fibre. Rank identifies structural visibility. The smallest positive eigenvalue describes conditioning. Both are calculated before respondents are recruited; statistical power is calculated separately.

The paper makes one methodological contribution. It turns a representation-sufficiency question into an auditable DCE design object. The certificate is deliberately placed beside the classical lack-of-fit design literature: Atkinson–Fedorov designs discriminate among specified rival models, while Goos, Kobilinsky, O’Brien, and Vandebroek and Gilmour–Trinca combine precision with model-robustness or lack-of-fit degrees of freedom. The present audit changes the object being certified. It conditions on the complete candidate menu summary, spans the response contrasts available inside each finite fibre, and reports a repair when the design has zero exposure. An unrestricted complete-fibre likelihood ratio, an all-profile CMH score, and a held-out residual learner are used as inferential comparators; the rank certificate is the pre-outcome design output.

## 2 Candidate preserving fibres

Let b(x) be the candidate alternative-level basis and let V₀(x; β) = b(x)ᵀβ. For a multinomial menu, φ(x) is the complete vector of candidate utility differences or an equivalent candidate menu prediction. A fibre F_z is the set of feasible menus satisfying φ(x) = z. A fibre is non-trivial only when different raw menus share the same candidate summary.

For a reflected binary task t = (A₊, A₋, B₊, B₋), the construction requires b(A₊) = b(A₋) and b(B₊) = b(B₋). The candidate utility difference is then identical under the two orientations for every β. A respondent-level orientation coin chooses one member of the reflected pair. In a multinomial task, the entire candidate menu vector must be preserved; matching one pairwise gap is insufficient.

For a local raw-coordinate departure h, the odd contrast is D_t(h) = ½{[h(A₊) − h(B₊)] − [h(A₋) − h(B₋)]}. If H is a declared finite departure space, stacking these contrasts over a task block S gives the exposure matrix D_S. This is a design relation, so it does not depend on an estimated coefficient vector. It is also a lack-of-fit object: when the raw support is saturated, the available contrast dimension is the number of positive-mass non-singleton fibre profiles minus the number of fibres. The certificate is useful only when this dimension is nonzero and the candidate summary is a genuine coarsening.

## 3 Sufficiency and the exposure certificate

The null leaves P(Y | φ(X)) unrestricted. For a local probability submodel p_η(x) = p₀(z) + η a_z h(x) + o(η), with z = φ(x), choose a full-row-rank contrast matrix C and write V_z = C[diag(p₀(z)) − p₀(z)p₀(z)ᵀ]Cᵀ and b_z = C a_z. The response metric is κ_z = b_zᵀ V_z⁻¹ b_z; V_z is positive definite on the chosen contrast coordinates. For a fibre draw X with conditional weights w_z, local information is

I_z(h) = κ_z Var(h(X) | z), and Iπ(h) = E_z[I_z(h)]. The factor 1/2 appears in the second-order Kullback–Leibler expansion, not in Fisher information.

A direction is structurally invisible when it is constant within every positive-mass fibre with κ_z > 0. A direction h(x) = q(φ(x)) has zero exposure because it belongs to the null. It is a functional-form question inside the candidate summary, not evidence that the summary discarded raw information. Discrete attribute levels require no attribute derivative; the local parameter is the departure amplitude η.

**Proposition 1 (continuous fibre information).** Let X have density ρ on a compact feasible set and let Z = φ(X), where φ is continuously differentiable with constant rank k on the interior of the support. Let pη(x) be a differentiable J-category choice law with p₀(x)=p₀(Z) and derivative d pη(x)/dη at η=0 equal to a(Z)h(x), with 1ᵀa(Z)=0. Choose a full-row-rank contrast matrix C of dimension J−1 and write V(Z)=C[diag(p₀(Z))−p₀(Z)p₀(Z)ᵀ]Cᵀ and b(Z)=Ca(Z). Define κ(Z)=b(Z)ᵀV(Z)⁻¹b(Z). The nuisance tangent space for the unrestricted response surface consists of conditional-mean-zero functions s(Y,Z) with E[s(Y,Z)\mid Z]=0. The projection of the departure score onto that space replaces h(X) by E[h(X)\mid Z]. After removing this nuisance component, the efficient per-observation information for η is

I_eff(h) = E[κ(Z)(h(X)−E[h(X)|Z])²] = E[κ(Z)Var(h(X)|Z)].

**Proof.** The categorical score is b(Z)ᵀV(Z)⁻¹C(Y−p₀(Z))h(X). Under the null, Y and X are conditionally independent given Z, so its L² projection onto the response-surface tangent space replaces h by E[h\mid Z]. The residual is h° = h−E[h\mid Z]. Conditional on Z, the response score has variance κ(Z), so the squared residual score has conditional variance κ(Z)Var(h\mid Z). Integrating over Z gives the result. The coarea formula supplies the conditional law on a regular fibre: its density is proportional to ρ(x)/Jφ(x) with respect to the (d−k)-dimensional Hausdorff measure. Thus the variance is computable from the feasible support and its design density. A direction h=q(φ) has h°=0 and belongs to the null. A positive-dimensional fibre with non-degenerate conditional mass has information when κ>0; a locally one-to-one map has singleton local fibres and zero information. This is a standard conditional-score projection used here to connect the finite certificate to continuous attributes; the paper does not claim the projection identity as a new semiparametric theorem. The statement is local and does not replace the exact finite-support construction for discrete attribute levels.

For example, let (T,C) be uniform on [0,1]² and let Z=T+2C. On a fibre z∈[0,1], C is uniform on [0,z/2]; on z∈[1,2], C is uniform on [(z−1)/2,z/2]; on z∈[2,3], C is uniform on [(z−1)/2,1]. For h(T,C)=T−C=z−3C, Var(h|Z=z)=3z²/16 for 0≤z≤1, 3/16 for 1≤z≤2, and 3(3−z)²/16 for 2≤z≤3. The candidate generalized-cost summary leaves a positive, explicitly calculable exposure for this departure. A log-price transform that is one-to-one in the only price coordinate gives singleton fibres and no such test content.

For a finite residual dictionary R_g on fibre g, let H_g = I − 1w_gᵀ and W_g = diag(w_g). The finite exposure matrix is E = Σ_g α_g κ_g R_gᵀ H_gᵀ W_g H_g R_g, where α_g is the fibre mass. A zero eigenvalue is a structural blind direction for the declared support. A positive eigenvalue identifies exposure; it does not establish finite-sample power.

## 3.1 Support complete finite certificate

A finite support permits a dictionary-free certificate. If fibre g has K_g feasible profiles, choose Q_g with orthonormal columns spanning the vectors orthogonal to 1. The saturated block is E_g^sat = α_g κ_g Q_gᵀ[diag(w_g) − w_g w_gᵀ]Q_g. The direct sum over all non-singleton fibres has dimension r_sat = Σ_g(K_g − 1). Positive mass on every feasible profile gives rank r_sat. Missing profiles remove the corresponding directions.

This certificate spans every non-constant response pattern on the declared support. It is stronger than a rank calculation for a selected polynomial dictionary, yet it remains a finite-support statement. It does not identify arbitrary functions outside the declared menu universe.

## 3.2 Structural visibility and power

Structural visibility and statistical power are separate. Rank and eigenvalues depend on the declared support, candidate summary, response metric, and feature scaling. Power additionally depends on η, sample size, respondent clustering, and the randomization reference. Increasing the number of respondents cannot recover a direction with zero structural exposure. A support change is required first.

## 4 Design search and support repair

The support optimizer has two stages. It first requires non-singleton fibres and full available rank. It then maximizes a conditioning criterion such as log det(E + εI) or the smallest positive eigenvalue, optionally recording candidate-index precision as a separate objective. The output includes profile weights, fibre masses, rank, eigenvalues, and the candidate-precision trade-off.

**Table 1. Exact finite-support counterexample.**

| Design | Var(φ) | Exposure rank | Minimum eigenvalue | Interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Candidate precision endpoint | 4.00 | 0 | 0.0000 | Precise candidate index; fibre blind |
| Support complete | 0.5333 | 4 | 0.1222 | All non-constant fibre contrasts exposed |

The counterexample uses profiles (a, b) ∈ {0, 1, 2}² and φ(a, b) = a + b. The endpoint design places half its mass on (0, 0) and half on (2, 2), so every supported fibre is a singleton. The support-complete allocation places positive mass on all nine profiles. It reduces candidate precision while creating four observable within-fibre contrasts.

The same operator gives a support-repair rule. For each declared departure h, calculate the spread of h within each fibre. If every spread is zero, the departure is unavailable on the current attribute domain. Search over admissible level expansions and select the smallest expansion with positive fibre spread. This is an audit output, not a second model-selection method.

## 5 Common-design audit

The finite rank formula is the familiar lack-of-fit dimension. Its practical value is shown by applying the same audit to a standard three-level orthogonal array and to locally D-efficient binary menus. The audit uses four raw coordinates `(time_A, cost_A, time_B, cost_B)` at levels 0, 1, and 2 and declares the candidate menu summary `φ = (time_A + cost_A, time_B + cost_B)`. The nine-run L9 array is the NIST/SEMATECH table with four three-level columns; all 24 assignments of its columns to the four menu coordinates were checked. Every assignment has exposure rank 0: each supported candidate vector occurs once, so the array has no within-fibre contrast even though its raw main-effect columns are balanced. A 12-task local D-exchange search for a raw linear logit gives rank 1 in all 50 starts for each of three prespecified priors. Adding one support-repair menu raises the L9 rank to 1 and the best D-exchange rank to 2. The full 3⁴ menu universe has rank 56. These are structural audits under the declared coarse summary, not claims that every orthogonal or D-efficient design is blind.

**Table 2. Common-design exposure audit.**

| Design | Tasks | Raw linear information | Exposure rank | Result |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | --- |
| NIST L9, all 24 column mappings | 9 | Balanced | 0 in all mappings | Candidate fibres are singletons |
| L9 plus one repair menu | 10 | Balanced | 1 | One within-fibre contrast becomes visible |
| D-exchange, 50 starts × 3 priors | 12 | Local logit D-search | 1 in all starts | Raw precision leaves most fibre directions unseen |
| Best D-exchange plus one repair | 13 | Local logit D-search | 2 | Support repair increases structural exposure |
| Full 3⁴ menu universe | 81 | Reference | 56 | Saturated finite contrast space |

The menu-level audit is the useful addition to the classical lack-of-fit analogy: it evaluates the complete candidate menu vector before outcomes are observed, records which raw designs have zero exposure, and returns an explicit feasible repair. It does not claim that the rank identity itself is new. The locked implementation is `analysis/common_dce_design_exposure_audit.py`; NIST's L9 table is cited as the external design reference.

## 6 Smart-device design

The substantive testbed uses six attributes: use-management mode, intelligence, professional support, clinical evidence, data management, and price. The candidate basis is b(x) = (m + s, i + e, d, q). Raw profiles are grouped by this vector and reflected into candidate-preserving tasks. The full structural pool contains 1,024 reflections. The declared local directions are total quadratic curvature, a midpoint mode-support interaction, and an intelligence-cloud interaction.

The full pool has residual rank 3. A three-task subset already reaches rank 3; a five-task maximin block has residual information eigenvalues 1.648, 6.063, and 7.352 under the declared normalization. An endpoint mode-support interaction is constant on the original level domain, so it is blind. Adding level 3 is the smallest tested support repair that creates fibre spread 1.

**Table 3. Smart-device structural checks.**

| Check | Result | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Full reflected-task pool | 1,024 tasks; rank 3 | All declared directions available |
| Three-task algebraic block | Rank 3; eigenvalues 0.483, 2.217, 5.055 | Minimal full-rank block in the pool |
| Five-task maximin block | Rank 3; eigenvalues 1.648, 6.063, 7.352 | Better conditioning after rank is met |
| Endpoint mode-support direction | Blind at levels 0, 1, 2 | Support repair required |
| Repair | Add level 3 | Positive fibre spread becomes feasible |

## 7 Simulation evidence

The finite-support benchmark uses 400 observations per replication and conditional randomization within each fibre. The support-complete score is compared with an unrestricted complete-fibre likelihood ratio, an all-profile generalized CMH score, and a held-out random-forest residual score. The hidden direction is constructed to be orthogonal to a two-feature dictionary while remaining exposed in the full fibre contrast space. The dictionary is retained as a diagnostic control, not as the main comparator.

**Table 4. Five-hundred-replication comparator benchmark (n = 400, η = 0.45).**

| Condition | Support-complete score | Generalized CMH | Complete-fibre LR | Held-out ML residual |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Null | 0.046 | 0.046 | 0.046 | 0.042 |
| Aligned departure | 1.000 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 0.998 |
| Interaction departure | 1.000 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 0.994 |
| Hidden quadratic | 1.000 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 0.992 |
| Hidden orthogonal direction | 1.000 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 0.998 |

Entries are rejection proportions at 0.05. The generalized CMH and complete-fibre LR both use every supported raw profile within each fibre; the former is the stratified score form and the latter is the unrestricted fibre-interaction deviance. The ML column uses 499 held-out profile draws per replication and is an exploratory predictive comparator. The null rows are conditional-randomization calibration results. They do not imply a new likelihood family or human-data evidence. The earlier two-feature dictionary is retained as a negative control in the locked legacy file; it is not the main baseline.

The locked `support_complete_vs_saturated_benchmark_round32_*` files are historical oracle-baseline diagnostics: they compare the proposed score with a saturated profile likelihood against the known simulated p₀. They are retained for provenance and are not used as the primary sufficiency comparison. The unrestricted-fibre comparison in Table 4 is the relevant calibration result. It makes the inferential boundary visible: the score and generalized CMH have the same full finite contrast target, while the complete-fibre LR is the corresponding deviance form. The reported settings show no meaningful separation between the score and LR; a claim of power dominance would require a separate, prespecified alternative grid. The added contribution is the pre-outcome rank certificate and the support optimizer that tells an analyst when the saturated alternative has no structural content.

## 7.1 Baseline comparators and power planning

The generalized CMH baseline uses all supported profile contrasts in every fibre and applies a stratified score to the binary outcome. The complete-interaction LR compares one unrestricted Bernoulli probability per raw profile with one unrestricted probability per fibre; its null is the same conditional-sufficiency relation. For a binary response the two tests are asymptotically equivalent quadratic/deviance views, so they serve as calibration comparators. The ML residual baseline fits a forest on a respondent-level training split and evaluates a frozen held-out residual log score under within-fibre profile draws. These baselines address standard lack-of-fit routes; the support-complete certificate determines in advance whether the design exposes the full finite alternative space.

Power is indexed by the departure amplitude η and respondent count n. The release includes `results/support_complete_power_curve.csv`, with η ∈ {0, 0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.20, 0.30, 0.45} and n ∈ {200, 400, 800}; all cells use 500 replications and 499 conditional randomization draws. Wilson intervals are stored with each rejection rate. The curve is the relevant evidence for non-ceiling performance: at η = 0.10 the support-score rejection rates are 0.212, 0.320, and 0.616 for n = 200, 400, and 800; at η = 0.20 they are 0.622, 0.930, and 1.000. The 1.000 values in Table 4 correspond to a deliberately strong η = 0.45 boundary condition.

## 7.2 Multinomial and nuisance controls

The multinomial implementation preserves the complete menu vector and uses two independent probability contrasts for each three-alternative arm. The residual rank is 3 and the smallest residual eigenvalue is 1.203. The nuisance-orthogonal extension projects declared framing and scale tangents with the full menu covariance metric. It is a control for a specified nuisance span; it does not grant immunity to unlisted process changes.

## 7.3 Questionnaire-level power planning

The respondent-facing instrument assigns one version to each respondent and contains eight choice tasks. Its planning calculation uses the respondent as the randomization unit. The script `analysis/questionnaire_cluster_power.py` simulates multinomial A/B/opt-out responses with respondent-level random utility components, keeps the eight-task cluster intact, and applies a respondent-level permutation Wald statistic to the task-by-choice response vector. The hidden departure changes the squared raw-level contrast while preserving the candidate vector; η = 0 is the conditional-sufficiency null in this planning model.

The 200-replication grid uses 499 complete-randomization draws per replication. At η = 0, rejection rates are 0.030, 0.045, and 0.050 for 200, 400, and 800 respondents. At η = 0.20 they are 0.100, 0.245, and 0.420; at η = 0.30 they are 0.165, 0.520, and 0.845. These values are planning outputs under the declared utility and random-effects calibration. They do not establish the effect size in a target population and they do not replace a comprehension pilot, ethics review, or preregistration. The earlier single-response curve remains a finite-support benchmark; it is not used to size the eight-task field instrument.

## 8 External implementation check

The public Swissmetro purpose-1/3 data provide 6,768 observations from 752 respondents. We use them as an implementation check for respondent grouping and multinomial model fitting. An additive model and an alternative-specific time and cost model are estimated on the public data. The structured model reduces held-out log loss from 0.8045 to 0.7686. This public file does not contain the candidate-preserving randomization needed for a human-data sufficiency test, so the result is descriptive and does not establish a mechanism or a causal effect.

**Table 5. Swissmetro implementation check.**

| Observations | Respondents | Base loss | Structured loss | Mean path gap | Max path gap |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 6,768 | 752 | 0.8045 | 0.7686 | 0.0647 | 0.0757 |

The second public check uses the `mlogit` Electricity DCE. It contains 4,308 choice tasks from 361 respondents, four alternatives per task, and 62 distinct complete menus. Five-fold respondent-grouped validation gives a linear log score of −1.156620 and a quadratic expansion of −1.156442. The effective alternative-difference ranks are 6 and 9; the corresponding naive within-sample LR reference is p = 0.0264. Because the archive repeats respondents and has no candidate-preserving assignment, this p-value is descriptive and is not treated as calibrated evidence. Repeated menus in this archive do not create candidate-preserving assignment, so they are not treated as a sufficiency test.

A separate Train vehicle archive provides 1,484 tasks from 100 respondents. Five-fold respondent-grouped validation gives a linear log score of −0.948173 and a quadratic expansion of −0.945571; the effective ranks are 7 and 13, with a naive LR p-value of 0.0021. The richer type-interaction and full specifications have effective ranks 9 and 15, so raw column counts are not used for LR degrees of freedom. These two public checks show why predictive specification comparisons belong in the external-validation section, while the candidate-preserving DCE remains the evidence needed for a human-data rejection claim.

## 9 Relation to nearby work and claim boundary

Assisted specification methods search across candidate utility forms. Misspecification tests compare outcomes with a fitted model or a broad MNL restriction. Fok and Paap develop composite-likelihood, Hausman, and GMM overidentification tests for MNL misspecification and IIA; those tests are the closest recent Journal of Choice Modelling benchmark, while the present null conditions on a complete candidate menu vector and leaves the response surface indexed by that vector unrestricted. The two diagnostics answer different questions and should be compared in a future Monte Carlo extension. Atkinson–Fedorov T-optimal designs discriminate among specified rival response models; Goos et al. develop model-robust and model-sensitive criteria that combine bias and lack-of-fit considerations; Gilmour and Trinca show how compound criteria preserve precision while retaining residual degrees of freedom; Wiens constructs maximin designs for lack-of-fit tests in binary response models. These are the correct design precedents for the present work. The present audit addresses a narrower object: whether raw coordinates discarded by a declared candidate menu summary have nonzero within-fibre exposure on the support the experiment creates. Its increment is the fibre-conditioned, full-menu certificate and its support-repair output. It does not claim a new lack-of-fit principle.

The method should be presented as a design-based finite-support audit. It should not be described as the first conditional-randomization test, a universal MNL misspecification test, or a nonparametric identification theorem. The saturated-LR comparison prevents an inflated novelty claim. The useful distinction is that the rank and support-repair output are available before outcome data and can reveal a zero-information design that ordinary precision criteria accept. The literature audit covered Crossref, publisher pages, open repositories, and broad web searches; direct Scopus and Google Scholar result pages were not consistently accessible, so no database-complete priority claim is made.

The separate policy-path audit supplies a common-mode negative control. Two models can agree on observed support and share an omitted off-support term. That result explains why model agreement cannot certify counterfactual correctness; it is not a second headline method.

## 10 Discussion and conclusion

A DCE can be precise for a candidate utility index and still be unable to criticize the index as a representation of the raw attributes. The fibre audit makes this distinction concrete. It preserves the complete candidate menu prediction, measures variation after conditioning on that prediction, and reports the support rank before estimation. The finite certificate identifies blind directions, while the optimizer supplies an explicit support repair or a power-planning input.

The scope is deliberately finite. A saturated raw basis has singleton fibres and no test content. A direction that is a function of φ belongs to the null. A rejection identifies a failure of the conditional relation on the declared support; it does not identify one psychological mechanism. A randomized candidate-preserving DCE is needed for a human-data rejection claim. The present simulations and Swissmetro run establish a reproducible method-and-design contribution with a transparent boundary.

The primary finite-support benchmark uses 500 replications, 400 observations, and 499 conditional randomization draws for the four-way comparator table.
